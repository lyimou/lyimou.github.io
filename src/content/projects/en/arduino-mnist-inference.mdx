## What it is

The idea was to take a model that normally lives on a PC and shrink it until it fits inside an 8-bit microcontroller — then prove it works by feeding the board images one at a time and scoring the answers.

Three things had to happen in order:

1. **Shrink the input.** Full 28×28 grayscale digits are far too much data for an Arduino's flash. Images are downscaled to 14×14 and binarised, giving 196 bits per digit.
2. **Shrink the model.** A small dense network (`Dense(8, sigmoid, MaxNorm(3))` → `BatchNormalization` → `Dense(10)`) trained in Keras, then quantised to `int8` with one scale factor per weight matrix.
3. **Move the weights and the protocol to the board.** Weights are emitted as `PROGMEM` C arrays; the PC sends a 196-character `0`/`1` string over serial and reads back `Prediction:<digit>`.

All classification happens on the Arduino. The PC only prepares the input and grades the output.

## The measurement pipeline

The part I would point at first is not the model — it is the harness around it.

`CNN_3_serial.py` opens the serial port, waits for a `READY` handshake, flushes the buffer before every image, re-opens the port if a read fails, and writes one row per image to a CSV with `image_index, true_label, predicted_label, is_correct`. That is what turns "the model works" into a number.

## Where it stands

**On-device accuracy is not verified.** The hardware run never wrote a result file, so no figure for the Arduino itself appears here — only the two Python-side numbers above, both reproducible from the committed CSVs.

There is also a known inconsistency I chose not to hide: the committed `arduino/cnn_int8/arduino_weights.h` declares `HIDDEN_SIZE 16` with float32 weights, while `CNN_small.py` exports 8 units as int8. The two were generated at different points in the project, so the header must be regenerated before flashing that sketch.

## Why the accuracy is not higher

~75% is well below what this approach could reach, and the pipeline says why:

- **14×14 nearest-neighbour downscaling destroys strokes.** Thin or slanted digits lose the detail that distinguishes them.
- **A fixed threshold at 128 throws away grayscale.** Anti-aliased edges carry real information that binarisation discards.
- **The baseline's hidden layer is not trained at all.** It generates weights from a chaotic map, so it cannot adapt to the data.

## What I learned

- **Quantisation is where the accuracy goes**, but the normalisation bounds and the input threshold mattered more to the final number than the network size.
- **Serial I/O is a state machine, not a function call.** Buffer resets, partial reads, timeouts and re-opening the port after a failure were the bulk of the debugging — not the model.
- **Flash is the real constraint.** Deciding what belongs in `PROGMEM`, and in what form, is what determines whether the network fits.
- **Input representation beats depth.** Two quite different architectures landed in the same accuracy band, because both were fed the same lossy 196-bit input.
- **Keep one source of truth for generated artefacts.** The header/exporter mismatch above exists precisely because they drifted apart.
